Four Lenses for Thinking Through an Ethical Dilemma

Most real ethical disagreements aren't about facts — they're about which framework people are unconsciously applying. Knowing the frameworks makes the disagreement legible.

Utilitarianism judges an action by its consequences: the right choice is whichever produces the greatest overall wellbeing. It's the framework behind most cost-benefit analysis and public-health policy, and it's also the framework behind the classic "trolley problem" used to test intuitions about self-driving car crash algorithms — should a car be programmed to minimize total harm even if that means acting against its own passenger?

Deontology, associated with philosopher Immanuel Kant, judges an action by whether it follows a moral rule, regardless of outcome — lying is wrong even if a particular lie would produce a better result. This is the intuition behind data-privacy law that protects consent as a right, not just something to be traded off against convenience.

Virtue ethics, tracing back to Aristotle, asks not "what rule applies" but "what would a person of good character do here." It's less about calculating an answer and more about cultivating judgment — relevant to debates about whether AI systems should be designed to optimize a metric at all, versus built to act more like a careful, well-intentioned professional would.

Care ethics centers relationships and context over abstract universal rules, asking what response best honors the specific people and dependencies involved. It's increasingly cited in debates about eldercare robotics and AI companionship, where the "right" answer depends heavily on the particular person's circumstances.

Applied to modern flashpoints — algorithmic bias in hiring software, gene-editing embryos with CRISPR, government use of facial recognition — these four lenses rarely agree completely, which is exactly why they're useful together: disagreement between frameworks is often a more honest signal than false consensus from only using one.
