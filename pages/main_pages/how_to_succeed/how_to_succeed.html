How to Succeed

Motivation is unreliable. Systems aren't. Here's what research on habits, goals, and skill-building actually says works.

Set systems, not just goals

A goal ("run a marathon") defines an outcome. A system ("run three times a week, rain or shine") defines the behavior that produces the outcome. Research on goal-setting, going back to Locke and Latham's work on goal theory, shows that specific, challenging-but-achievable goals outperform vague ones — but goals alone don't create action. The behaviors you repeat daily are what actually compound; the goal mainly tells you which direction to point the system.

Make the first step almost too easy

Habit-formation research (notably work summarized by psychologist BJ Fogg and popularized more broadly) consistently finds that the biggest barrier to a new habit is friction at the start, not lack of willpower. Shrinking the initial version of a habit — "put on running shoes" instead of "run five miles" — dramatically increases the odds you actually begin, and starting is usually what determines whether the full behavior happens at all.

Track the process, not just the result

Results are often lagging and noisy — a good process can still produce a bad week. Tracking input measures (workouts completed, pages written, deep-work hours logged) gives faster feedback than tracking only outcome measures (weight, revenue, exam score), and faster feedback loops are one of the most consistent findings in skill-acquisition research for accelerating improvement.

Deliberate practice beats raw repetition

K. Anders Ericsson's research on expertise (the origin of the "10,000 hours" idea, though the number itself is often oversimplified) found that expertise comes from deliberate practice specifically: working just past your current skill level, with immediate feedback, on a narrowly defined weakness — not simply logging hours doing what you're already good at. Passive repetition plateaus; targeted, uncomfortable practice keeps improving.

Protect recovery like it's part of the plan

Both athletic training research and cognitive-performance studies converge on the same point: performance gains happen during recovery, not just during effort. Overtraining research shows that skipping rest doesn't just fail to add extra progress — past a certain point it actively erodes it, through fatigue, injury, and burnout. Scheduling rest deliberately, rather than treating it as an interruption to "real" progress, tends to produce faster net gains over months, not slower ones.

Environment design beats self-control

Studies on self-control — including research summarized in Wendy Wood's work on habit formation — find that people who appear highly disciplined usually aren't resisting temptation more successfully than anyone else; they've simply arranged their environment to encounter less of it in the first place. Removing a distraction from arm's reach reliably beats trying to out-willpower it every single time.
